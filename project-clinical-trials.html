The Question

Three questions, one narrative

I wanted to answer three things: where does Alzheimer's drug development actually fail, has the field's research mix changed in response, and are the trials that do run getting faster or slower? I framed all three questions before writing a single query, then let the pipeline's output tell me the answers rather than fitting the data to something I already believed.

Pipeline & Data Model

API → BigQuery → dbt → analytical SQL

The extraction script pulls the full Alzheimer's-scoped dataset from ClinicalTrials.gov's public API. I deliberately pulled full study records instead of a field-filtered subset, so the nested JSON stayed genuinely messy and the dbt staging layer had real flattening work to do, rather than getting handed clean data by the extraction step. Raw JSON lands in BigQuery first, then moves through three dbt layers:

LayerModelPurpose
Stagingstg_studiesFlattens nested JSON into one clean row per study
Intermediateint_studies_categorizedBusiness-logic categorization: outcome status, phase grouping, duration fields
Martsfct_studiesThe stable, query-ready fact table — analysis and BI tools query only this

dbt tests enforce not_null and unique on nct_id, plus category validity, across all three layers. One distinction I made a point of keeping: an empty phases array (usually an observational study) is not the same thing as an array containing the literal string "NA" (an interventional study where the phase field just wasn't applicable). Lumping both into one "unknown" bucket would have thrown away real information.

Question 1

Where do Alzheimer's trials actually fail?

I used a window function to rank termination rate by sponsor class and phase, limited to trials with a known outcome. Still-active trials get excluded here since we don't know how they'll end yet.

Sponsor classPhasenTermination rate
IndustryPhase 135711.5%
IndustryPhase 231624.4%
IndustryPhase 319035.3%
IndustryPhase 44314.0%
Academic (Other)Phase 115618.6%

Industry-sponsored trials get riskier at every phase, climbing from 11.5% termination at Phase 1 to 35.3% by Phase 3, the field's most expensive and demanding stage. Phase 4 drops back down, but that's survivorship: only drugs that already got approved make it to a post-approval trial. Academic trials do the opposite. They peak at Phase 1 (18.6%) and get safer from there, which points to a different failure mode entirely — funding falling through rather than a drug not working.

Federal, NIH, and other-government sponsors have small samples in several phase groups (as few as 5–13 trials), so I'd treat those rates as noisier than the Industry and Academic numbers above.

Question 2

Has the field shifted away from classic drug trials?

Here I compared phase-classified interventional trials, the "classic" drug trial, against non-phase-classified studies (mostly observational or biomarker work), both over time and on a few other characteristics.

EraPhase-classified share

Sponsor classNon-phase avg. enrollmentPhase-classified avg. enrollmentRatio

The field has moved toward observational research. Phase-classified trials made up 60–67% of the field at their 2006–2009 peak; since 2016 that's settled at 25–36%, with no sign of recovering. Non-phase trials also enroll 15–35x more participants, which makes sense once you think about it: observational studies just track people over time, while interventional trials need per-participant dosing and safety monitoring, so they stay small by design. NIH- and network-sponsored non-phase trials run especially long, 3,194 and 4,643 days on average (8.7 and 12.7 years), which lines up with the kind of decades-long cohort studies NIH funds in this space — ADNI being the well-known example.

I want to flag this as my interpretation, not something the data proves outright: if industry trials keep failing hardest at the expensive, late-stage Phase 3, a shift toward foundational biomarker and observational work is exactly what I'd expect the field to do instead of repeating costly late-phase attempts. It's a plausible story that connects back to Question 1, but it's a story, not a proof.

Question 3

Are the trials that do run getting faster?

Median time-to-completion by start-year cohort, limited to trials that have both a start and completion date on record.

Median trial duration has roughly halved since the 2010–2014 peak: 1,800–2,200 days down to 641–655 by 2024–2025, and recent years have decent sample sizes (218–252 trials) so I trust this trend. I checked whether this was just an artifact of the Question 2 shift, and it isn't — phase-classified and non-phase trials run about the same length within each sponsor type (877 vs. 857 days for Industry, 1,272 vs. 1,201 for Academic). So the duration drop is its own thing. I can think of two explanations, neither of which I can prove from this data alone: biomarker-based patient enrichment (confirming amyloid-positive status before enrolling someone) letting trials wrap up faster with better-selected participants, or just growing urgency in the field given how much unmet need there still is.

Early years (1981–2005) and the tail year (2027) only have 1–4 trials each and aren't filtered the same way as the rest of the analysis, so I wouldn't read anything into them.

Key Findings

A coherent story, not three disconnected numbers

These three findings connect: drug development struggles hardest at the expensive, late Phase 3 stage, the field has responded by leaning into observational and biomarker research, and the trials that do still run have gotten noticeably faster over the past decade. I didn't decide on this narrative up front — it's what fell out of the queries once I ran them.

  • Industry-sponsored Alzheimer's trials fail most often at Phase 3 (35.3% termination). Academic trials fail earliest instead, at Phase 1 (18.6%), which looks more like a funding problem than an efficacy one.
  • The phase-classified share of AD trials has fallen by roughly half since its 2006–2009 peak, and it hasn't recovered since 2016. That's a real shift in how the field works, not noise.
  • Median trial duration has roughly halved since 2010–2014, and that holds up even after I controlled for the phase-classification shift. It's a separate trend, not a side effect.
  • I've tried to be upfront about confidence throughout — flagging small-sample sponsor classes and sparse early or tail years instead of presenting every number as equally solid.
