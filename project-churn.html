Predicting Churn, From Signal to Retention Decision

Can churn be predicted well enough to actually act on, and under what conditions does acting on it beat doing something simpler? A model alone doesn't answer that. Most of the work in this project went into the decision layered on top of it, not the model itself.

The question

Two questions, not one

Data science question: can I reliably identify customers at elevated churn risk? Decision-science question: if I can, does that actually improve a retention strategy once I'm honest about the assumptions behind it? Most churn projects stop at the first question and call it done. I spent most of my time on the second one.

Exploratory Analysis

Where the signal actually is

7,043 Telco customers, minus 11 dropped for missing billing data (zero-tenure customers who'd never actually been billed), leaves 7,032. I tested every relationship below statistically instead of eyeballing a chart: Mann-Whitney U for the continuous variables (churned and stayed distributions aren't normal, so a t-test would be the wrong call), chi-square for categorical associations, and Cramér's V to put those associations on a common 0–1 scale so I could rank them.

Tenure and price turned out to be the strongest individual predictors. Median tenure among churned customers is 10 months, versus 38 months for customers who stayed (p = 6.0×10⁻²¹¹). Median monthly charges: $79.65 versus $64.45 (p = 8.5×10⁻⁵⁴). Both gaps are large in practical terms too, not just statistically significant because the sample is big.

Feature Engineering

Built to survive contact with a train/test split

Every engineered feature uses fixed rules or per-row arithmetic — tenure buckets, spend-per-tenure ratio, a corrected service count, a security/support flag, an electronic-check flag. None of it depends on a dataset-level statistic, so it's safe to compute before splitting the data. The two things that do need a statistic, a spend-median threshold and one-hot encoding, get fit on training data only and then applied unchanged to test.

I found a bug worth writing up rather than quietly fixing. The first version of my "number of services" feature counted across all 8 service columns, phone service included. That conflated "has internet, declined every add-on" with "has no internet at all," and it broke monotonicity — churn rate went 0.44 → 0.21 → 0.33 → 0.36 as the count went up, which makes no sense for a feature that's supposed to signal loyalty. Restricting the count to internet-dependent add-ons, and only computing it for customers who actually have internet, fixed it. Churn rate now drops cleanly from 52% at 0 services to 5% at 6.

I dropped gender and PhoneService entirely instead of encoding them. The EDA's Cramér's V ranking already put both at essentially zero (0.008 and 0.011), so keeping them would only give the model noise to learn to ignore.

All three models clear the baseline by a wide margin and end up clustered tightly together. Logistic Regression actually edges out XGBoost on AUC-ROC (0.837 vs. 0.828), which I think matters: the simplest, fully-interpretable model isn't meaningfully worse than the more complex ones here. I still carried XGBoost forward for the threshold-locking and SHAP work below, mostly to show that tooling, but the honest takeaway is that model complexity bought very little on this dataset.

I locked the threshold without ever touching the test set. I swept 5-fold cross-validated out-of-fold predictions on training data only across a range of thresholds and picked the one that maximized F1 (0.50, OOF F1 = 0.631). The test set got used exactly once after that, for a single final evaluation. This fixes a leakage bug from an earlier draft, where I'd picked the threshold by maximizing F1 on the test set itself, then reported results on that same test set — a mistake I only caught by re-reading my own pipeline critically.

Calibration matters here because these probabilities drive real targeting decisions later on. If the model says 70% churn risk, that should mean roughly 70% of customers scored that way actually churn — not just that they rank higher than everyone else.

Interpretation

What the model is actually using — and where it's blind

The model's blind spot sits right on top of its strongest signal. Subgroup error analysis on the locked-threshold predictions shows the false-negative rate (missed churners) climbing from 10.7% for 0–6 month customers up to 58.3% for 4+ year customers. Two-year contract holders get missed 100% of the time — the model almost never flags anyone in that group as high-risk, because long tenure and long contracts are such strong loyalty signals that it can't see the rare customer in that bucket who actually leaves. That's not a bug I need to fix quietly. It's a real limitation of the approach, and I'd rather say so than pretend it isn't there.

Retention Strategy

From a probability to a targeting decision

I compared six strategies: contact nobody, contact everyone, the model's locked threshold, and three fixed-capacity strategies (top 5% / 10% / 20% by predicted risk). The fixed-capacity strategies exist because that's how a real retention campaign actually works — a team usually has budget for N outreach calls, not "however many the threshold happens to flag that month."

I've stated the business assumptions explicitly instead of burying them in the numbers: CLV = $1,200, offer cost = $50, retention success rate = 30%. These are illustrative figures I picked, not real company data.

The conclusion doesn't hold under every assumption, and I think that's the actual finding here, not a weakness of the analysis. Run the sensitivity analysis and "contact everyone" wins instead once CLV rises to $1,600, offer cost drops to $30, or the retention success rate reaches 40%. Solving for the break-even point directly, holding CLV and cost at base case, gives ~39%: below that retention rate, blanket contact wins; above it, targeted contact wins.

Key Findings

What this project actually shows

  • Tenure and monthly charges are the two strongest individual predictors of churn, both with a wide statistical margin behind them.
  • Three well-tuned models land within a point of each other on AUC-ROC. Logistic Regression, the simplest one, holds its own against XGBoost rather than losing to it.
  • Locking the threshold via cross-validation, without ever touching test data, is the difference between a result I can defend and one that's quietly optimistic.
  • The model's biggest blind spot — long-tenure, long-contract churners — sits right where its predictive signal is strongest. I'd rather state that than leave it for someone else to find.
  • Model-based retention targeting beats contacting every customer, but only above roughly a 39% retention success rate. Below that, plain blanket outreach wins. It's a conditional result, not an absolute one.
