The question

Two questions, not one

Data science question: can customers at elevated churn risk be identified reliably? Decision-science question: can those predictions improve a retention strategy, and does that hold up once the assumptions behind it are stated honestly? Most churn projects stop at the first question. This one spends most of its effort on the second.

Exploratory Analysis

Where the signal actually is

7,043 Telco customers, minus 11 dropped for missing billing data (customers with zero tenure who'd never been billed), leaving 7,032. Every relationship below was tested statistically rather than eyeballed: Mann-Whitney U for the continuous variables, since churned and stayed distributions aren't normal and a t-test would be the wrong tool, chi-square for categorical associations, and Cramér's V to rank those associations on a common 0–1 scale.

Tenure and price are the strongest individual predictors. Median tenure among churned customers is 10 months, versus 38 months among those who stayed (p = 6.0×10⁻²¹¹). Median monthly charges: $79.65 versus $64.45 (p = 8.5×10⁻⁵⁴). Both gaps are large by any practical standard — this isn't just statistical significance showing up on a big sample.

Feature Engineering

Built to survive contact with a train/test split

Every engineered feature here uses fixed rules or per-row arithmetic: tenure buckets, spend-per-tenure ratio, a corrected service count, a security/support flag, an electronic-check flag. None of it depends on a dataset-level statistic, so it's safe to compute before any split. Two things that do need a statistic, a spend-median threshold and one-hot encoding, are fit on training data only and applied unchanged to test.

A bug worth documenting, not hiding: the first version of a "number of services" feature counted across all 8 service columns, including phone service. That conflated "has internet, declined every add-on" with "has no internet at all," and broke monotonicity: churn rate went 0.44 → 0.21 → 0.33 → 0.36 as the count increased. Restricting the count to internet-dependent add-ons, computed only for customers who have internet, fixed it. Churn rate now drops cleanly from 52% at 0 services to 5% at 6.

gender and PhoneService were dropped entirely rather than encoded. The EDA's own Cramér's V ranking found both at essentially zero (0.008 and 0.011), so keeping them would only have added noise features for the model to learn to ignore.

All three models clear the baseline by a wide margin and cluster tightly together. Logistic Regression edges out XGBoost on AUC-ROC here (0.837 vs. 0.828), which matters: the simplest, fully-interpretable model isn't meaningfully worse than the more complex ones on this dataset. XGBoost was carried forward for the threshold-locking and SHAP interpretation below, mainly to demonstrate that tooling. The plain conclusion is that model complexity bought very little here.

The threshold was locked without ever touching the test set. 5-fold cross-validated out-of-fold predictions on training data only were swept across thresholds, and the one maximizing F1 was picked (0.50, OOF F1 = 0.631). The test set was then used exactly once, for a single final evaluation. This fixes a leakage bug from an earlier draft, which had chosen the threshold by maximizing F1 on the test set itself, then reported results on that same test set.

Calibration matters because these probabilities drive real targeting decisions below. A predicted 70% churn risk should mean roughly 70% of similarly-scored customers actually churn.

Interpretation

What the model is actually using — and where it's blind

The model's blind spot is exactly where its strongest signal lives. Subgroup error analysis on the locked-threshold predictions shows the false-negative rate (missed churners) climbing from 10.7% for 0–6 month customers to 58.3% for 4+ year customers. Two-year contract holders have a 100% false-negative rate: the model almost never flags anyone in that group as high-risk, because long tenure and long contracts are such strong loyalty signals that it misses the rare customer there who does leave. This isn't a bug. It's a defensible limitation, stated plainly rather than glossed over.

Retention Strategy

From a probability to a targeting decision

Six strategies compared: contact nobody, contact everyone, the model's locked threshold, and three fixed-capacity strategies (top 5% / 10% / 20% by predicted risk). The fixed-capacity strategies reflect the realistic shape of an actual retention campaign, where a team usually has budget for N outreach calls, not "however many the threshold happens to flag."

Business assumptions are stated explicitly rather than buried inside the numbers: CLV = $1,200, offer cost = $50, retention success rate = 30%. These are illustrative figures, not real company data.

The conclusion isn't robust to every assumption, and that's the actual finding. The sensitivity analysis shows "contact everyone" winning instead when CLV rises to $1,600, when offer cost drops to $30, or when the retention success rate reaches 40%. The break-even retention rate, holding CLV and cost at base case, is ~39%: below it, blanket contact wins, and above it, targeted contact wins.

Key Findings

What this project actually shows

  • Tenure and monthly charges are the two strongest individual predictors of churn, both by a wide statistical margin.
  • Three well-tuned models land within 1 point of each other on AUC-ROC. The simplest one, Logistic Regression, is competitive with XGBoost here, not clearly worse.
  • A cross-validation-locked threshold, chosen without touching test data, is the methodological difference between a defensible result and an optimistically biased one.
  • The model's biggest blind spot, long-tenure and long-contract churners, is exactly where its strongest predictive signal also lives. That's a stated limitation, not a hidden one.
  • Model-based retention targeting beats contacting every customer, but only above a ~39% retention success rate. Below that, simpler blanket outreach wins. The claim here is conditional, not absolute.
