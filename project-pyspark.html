Can an independently built pipeline — no shortcuts, validated at every step against real ground truth — correctly rediscover a rare biological population using nothing but marker genes and unsupervised clustering? The Question

Two questions, building on each other

First: can a pipeline built entirely from scratch — QC, normalization, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, clustering, marker-based annotation — correctly recover a specific, known rare cell population, validated against a published dataset's own ground-truth labels rather than just producing a plausible-looking result? Second, and the more interesting one: does that reliability hold at the sample sizes typical of real single-cell studies (a few thousand to tens of thousands of cells), or does it only work at this dataset's unusually large full scale (~89,000 cells)?

Data & Pipeline

SCP1290 — Di Bella et al. 2021, Nature

Single-cell RNA sequencing of the developing mouse cerebral cortex, spanning 13 developmental timepoints (E10 through P4). 98,047 cells by 19,712 genes at load, chosen specifically because the study's own published cell-type annotations — including Cajal-Retzius cells, a genuinely rare population that plays a key early role in cortical layering — provide real ground truth to validate against, not just a plausible-looking cluster.

Architecture, deliberately split between distributed and local computation: ingestion, QC aggregation, and highly-variable-gene selection run in Spark across the full dataset — the genuinely heavy, distributed-appropriate work. Once the feature-reduced data comfortably fits in memory (88,968 cells × 1,560 features after HVG selection, ~91.5% sparse), PCA and clustering run locally with scikit-learn. That's a deliberate engineering decision, not a shortcut — Spark ML's model-size guardrails made distributed PCA and clustering actively counterproductive at this scale, and there's no reason to fight that when the data no longer needs it.

Quality Control

A real catch, and QC thresholds calibrated against evidence, not defaults

The deposited matrix turned out to be already log-normalized, not raw counts. Comparing computed per-cell totals against the metadata's published nCount_RNA showed the zero-pattern matched exactly but the magnitudes didn't (correlation only 0.797, non-integer values) — the signature of a log1p transform already applied. Re-normalizing on top of that would have silently double-transformed the data: no error, just wrong numbers everywhere downstream. Caught before it happened, not after.

Standard MAD-based thresholds (n_mads=5.0, within the typical 3–5 literature range) were cross-checked against the original authors' own "Low quality cells" and "Doublet" labels — a direct test of whether the threshold was actually catching anything real. It caught zero of 6,399 flagged cells. Tightened to n_mads=3.0 in response.

A second diagnosis mattered more than the threshold tightening: "Low quality" and "Doublet" are different failure modes. Doublets (two cells captured as one) often look perfectly healthy by depth metrics — what defines a doublet is a mixed transcriptional signature, not shallow sequencing. Rather than tighten a depth threshold further, the fix was switching to the dataset's own purpose-built Scrublet doublet score directly.

A third QC dimension — housekeeping genes detected per cell (n_hkgene) — was added only after testing whether it actually separated flagged cells from the rest.

Confirmed useful, it was then swept across a range of thresholds (2.0–6.0 MADs) to find the point that traded off junk-catch rate against Cajal-Retzius retention explicitly, rather than picking a threshold and hoping. Landed at 3.5 MADs: 54.8% combined catch rate on flagged cells, 89.3% CR-cell retention (57 of 532 CR cells lost in exchange for that filtering power).

HVG Selection & PCA

Feature selection and dimensionality reduction, each with a data-driven stopping point

Highly variable genes selected via binned dispersion z-score — the classic Seurat/scanpy method — rather than an arbitrary top-N. Genes are binned by mean expression first, since raw dispersion is confounded with expression level (highly-expressed genes are almost always more variable in absolute terms, which isn't the same as being informative); a gene only counts as an HVG if its dispersion is an outlier relative to other genes at a similar expression level. 1,560 genes selected at a z-score cutoff of 1.0.

PCA run via TruncatedSVD rather than textbook mean-centered PCA — a deliberate choice for sparse data specifically. Mean-centering would subtract a non-zero mean from every gene, turning every implicit zero into a small non-zero value and destroying the matrix's sparsity in the process. This is standard practice for sparse single-cell data, the same reason tools like scanpy default to a similar approach.

A subtlety worth stating rather than glossing over: uncentered SVD can produce an anomalous first component dominated by overall expression magnitude rather than genuine biological variation — nearly every cell projects similarly onto that direction, giving it a large singular value but low actual informativeness. Checked directly (comparing PC1's explained variance against PC2's, which should never be lower for correctly-ordered singular values) rather than assumed away. 20 principal components carried forward to clustering, explaining 42.2% of variance.

Clustering & Validation

Optimizing for one rare population, not general structure

A standard inertia/silhouette sweep favored k≈11–15 — the best overall clustering structure across roughly 25 published cell types at once. But resolving one population that makes up only ~0.5% of the dataset needs far finer resolution than whatever's optimal for the dataset as a whole: at low k, a rare population simply gets absorbed into whichever larger, coarser cluster it resembles most.

A systematic search measured this directly: for each k from 15 to 80, cluster, score every cluster by mean expression of four Cajal-Retzius markers (Reln, Trp73, Calb2, Tbr1), and record precision/recall/F1 for the top-scoring cluster against the published labels. F1 stayed weak (0.06–0.09) from k=15 through k=35, jumped sharply at k=40, and peaked at k=75 (single cluster: 77.7% precision, 46.3% recall, F1=0.580) — a real, evidence-based optimum for this specific question, not the generically "best" k for the dataset overall.

46.3% recall at k=75 was investigated, not accepted as a ceiling. Checking whether the missed CR cells were scattered randomly or concentrated in a second, nearby CR-enriched cluster found the latter — combining the top-2 CR-scoring clusters substantially recovered recall. The final answer is genuinely two related subclusters, not one: precision 55.2%, recall 93.3%, F1=0.694 at full scale. Worth stating explicitly which trade-off this favors — for a rare-cell discovery context, missing real CR cells is generally worse than tolerating some false positives in an exploratory cluster, which is why the combined-cluster answer is the one carried forward rather than the higher-precision single cluster.

Sampling Reliability

The headline finding: recall lies to you at small scale

The real question this project is built around: this dataset happens to be unusually large for a single study. Does detecting a population this rare stay reliable at the sample sizes most single-cell studies actually work with — a few thousand to tens of thousands of cells — or does it only work this well because of scale most studies don't have?

Method: draw 8 independent random subsamples at each of five sizes, re-run the exact same validated detection method (cluster, score by CR markers, take the top-2 scoring clusters) on each — not a simplified or different method, which would confound "does scale matter" with "did the method change." k scales proportionally with subsample size, using the same cells-per-cluster ratio as the full-scale k=75.

Recall stays deceptively high at small sample sizes while precision collapses. At 2,000–10,000 cells, recall holds at 65–78% — the method looks like it's successfully finding Cajal-Retzius cells. But precision at those same sizes is only 1.4%: the "candidate" cluster is really a broad, heterogeneous grab-bag that happens to contain most of the true CR cells buried among a much larger number of false positives. Anyone judging detection quality by recall alone — a common shortcut — would conclude the method works fine at small scale. It doesn't. This is the finding that matters most for anyone actually running rare-cell studies at typical sample sizes.

Key Findings

What this project actually shows

  • An independently built pipeline correctly rediscovers a genuinely rare (~0.5%) published cell population using only marker genes and unsupervised clustering — validated against real ground truth, not just a plausible-looking result.
  • Real methodological catches mattered as much as the final numbers: an already-normalized matrix that would have been silently double-transformed, a MAD threshold that literally caught 0% of author-flagged bad cells until recalibrated against evidence, and a doublet-detection fix based on understanding why depth-based filtering fundamentally can't catch doublets.
  • The clustering resolution that works best for the dataset overall (k≈11–15) is not the resolution that works best for isolating one rare population (k=75) — general clustering quality and rare-population resolution are different optimization targets, and conflating them would have missed the real answer.
  • Recall alone is a misleading signal for rare-cell detection reliability. At realistic single-cell study sample sizes (thousands, not tens of thousands of cells), recall looks fine (65–80%) while precision collapses to ~1%, meaning the method is really returning broad, non-specific clusters rather than a genuine rare-population signal.
